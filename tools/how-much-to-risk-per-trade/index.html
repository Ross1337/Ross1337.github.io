How Much Should I Risk Per Trade? The 1–2% Rule, Explained

There is a real answer, and it is a number decided in advance, not a feeling in the moment: risk a small, fixed percentage of your account on every trade. Here is why the number is so much smaller than most beginners expect, how it drives your risk of ruin and drawdown, and how to turn it into an actual position size.

Ask ten new traders how much they risk per trade and most cannot give you a number — they trade whatever size "feels right," which means it is really their emotions sizing the position. Yet this is the single most important decision in trading: get it right and a long losing streak is merely annoying; get it wrong and one cold spell ends the account before your edge ever gets to work. The good news is that the answer is simple, well-established and the same for almost everyone — risk a small, fixed percentage of your account, decided before you enter and held constant no matter how sure you feel. This guide explains what that percentage should be, why it is so small, how it connects to risk of ruin and drawdown, and how to convert it into a precise number of shares or contracts.

The short answer: risk a small, fixed percentage

There is a real answer to "how much should I risk per trade," and it is a number decided in advance, not a feeling in the moment: risk a small, fixed percentage of your account on every trade — for most traders between 0.5% and 2%, with 1% the common default. "Risk" here means what you lose if the trade hits its stop, not the size of the position. A $10,000 account risking 1% is putting $100 on the line per trade, even though the position itself might be worth several thousand dollars once the stop distance is factored in. And it should be a percentage, not a flat dollar figure, so the amount breathes with your account as it grows and shrinks — that comparison is its own topic. The part that matters here is the size of the number, and why it is so much smaller than most beginners reach for.

Why so small: losing streaks are longer than you think

The reason the number lives between 0.5% and 2% is not caution for its own sake — it is the arithmetic of losing streaks. Even a strategy that wins half its trades will, over a few hundred trades, throw runs of six, eight, ten losers in a row; that is ordinary variance, not a broken edge. Risk 1% and a ten-trade losing streak costs you roughly 10% — irritating, fully recoverable. Risk 5% and that same streak takes out around 40%, and now you need a +67% gain just to get back to even, because the deeper the hole the more disproportionate the climb out. Push to 10% per trade and a normal cold spell can end the account outright — that is risk of ruin, the probability a losing streak wipes you out before your edge ever pays off. The single biggest driver of risk of ruin is the percentage you risk per trade, which is exactly why keeping it small is the whole game.

Turning the percentage into an actual position size

A percentage is not a position size — you cannot place a trade with "1%." The bridge between the two is your stop distance. The formula is simple: position size = (account × risk %) ÷ (distance to your stop). Say you have a $10,000 account, risk 1% ($100), and your stop sits $0.50 below your entry — you can buy 200 shares, because 200 × $0.50 = $100 at risk. Move the stop to $1.00 away and the same $100 risk only buys 100 shares. Notice what this means: a wider stop forces a smaller position, a tighter stop allows a larger one — the dollar risk stays pinned at 1% either way. This is why your stop has to be placed on the chart first, by structure or volatility, and your size derived from it second — never the reverse. A position size calculator does this arithmetic for you so the number is exact every time.

The mistake: sizing up because you feel sure

The rule is easy to state and hard to keep, because the trades you feel most certain about are exactly the ones that tempt you to break it. "This setup is perfect — I'll risk 5% just this once." The problem is that conviction has almost no correlation with outcome: the market does not know or care how sure you feel, and the trade you were certain about loses about as often as any other. Risk discipline only works if it is constant — the entire point of a fixed percentage is that it removes the in-the-moment decision that emotion corrupts. The same trap shows up as revenge sizing (doubling up to win back a loss) and FOMO sizing (going big on a fast-moving name). All three are the same error: letting a feeling, rather than a rule, set the most important number in trading. Pick your percentage once, size every trade to it, and let the edge — not the adrenaline — do the work.

Frequently asked questions

As a beginner, risk 1% of your account per trade or less — many professionals never go above 2%, and starting lower while you learn is sensible. Risking 1% means a losing streak costs you a recoverable amount and gives you the runway to make mistakes, refine your process and discover whether you actually have an edge before the account is in danger. The temptation early on is to risk more to grow faster, but small accounts blow up from oversizing far more often than they stall from under-risking.
