How to Calculate Trading Expectancy

Expectancy is the single number that tells you whether a strategy makes money over the long run. Here is the formula, a worked example, and how to read the result — so you stop guessing and start measuring your edge.

Most traders obsess over win rate. But a strategy that wins 90% of the time can still bleed money, and one that wins 30% can be a goldmine. The number that settles the argument is expectancy: the average amount you can expect to win — or lose — per trade, over a large sample. Get it positive and time is on your side; let it slip negative and no amount of discipline will save the account. This guide shows you exactly how to calculate it, with a worked example you can follow on your own numbers.

What trading expectancy actually means

Expectancy is your expected value per trade — the average profit or loss you would book per trade if you repeated your strategy thousands of times. It rolls two things into one figure: how often you win, and how big your wins are versus your losses. A positive expectancy means that, on average, every trade you take adds money to your account; a negative one means every trade quietly subtracts. It is the trader’s version of a casino’s house edge — small per bet, decisive over volume. Crucially, it does not predict any single trade. It tells you the direction the account drifts once the law of large numbers takes over, which is the only thing that matters for a strategy you will run hundreds of times.

The expectancy formula

The standard formula is: Expectancy = (Win rate × Average win) − (Loss rate × Average loss). Win rate is the share of trades that make money, expressed as a decimal (a 45% win rate is 0.45). Loss rate is simply 1 − win rate. Average win is the mean profit of your winning trades; average loss is the mean loss of your losers, entered as a positive number. The result is a dollar figure: your expected profit per trade. Many traders also express it in “R” — multiples of the amount risked per trade — so a strategy can be compared regardless of position size. To get expectancy in R, use your average win and average loss measured in R instead of dollars.

A worked example

Say you take 100 trades. You win 40 of them (a 40% win rate, so loss rate 60%). Your average winner makes $300; your average loser costs $150. Plug it in: (0.40 × $300) − (0.60 × $150) = $120 − $90 = $30. Your expectancy is +$30 per trade. Over those 100 trades you would expect roughly $3,000 in profit — despite losing 60% of the time. That is the lesson expectancy teaches: a sub-50% win rate is perfectly profitable when your winners are large enough relative to your losers. Flip the averages — win $150, lose $300 — and the same 40% win rate gives (0.40 × $150) − (0.60 × $300) = −$120 per trade, a strategy that bleeds money no matter how disciplined you are.

How to read your expectancy — and make it reliable

Any positive expectancy is an edge; the bigger the number, the faster your account compounds. But two cautions. First, expectancy is only as trustworthy as the sample behind it — twenty trades prove nothing, because variance dominates small samples. You need a few hundred trades before the figure stabilizes. Second, a high expectancy paired with oversized positions can still ruin you: a positive edge does not guarantee survival through the losing streaks that variance produces. That is why expectancy pairs with risk of ruin — one tells you the strategy wins over time, the other tells you whether your sizing lets you live long enough to collect. Track every trade so the win rate and averages you feed the formula are measured facts, not hopeful guesses.

Frequently asked questions

Any positive expectancy is good — it means the strategy makes money on average. In R terms, an expectancy of 0.1R to 0.3R per trade is solid and realistic for many discretionary strategies; consistently above 0.5R is excellent. What matters more than the exact figure is that it stays positive across a large sample and that your position sizing lets you survive the variance along the way.
