Fixed Percentage vs Fixed Dollar Position Sizing

The two most common ways to decide how big each trade should be — and why one quietly compounds your account while the other slowly falls behind. Here is how each works, when to use it, and the version most professionals settle on.

Position sizing is the single most important decision in trading, and most people never make it on purpose — they just trade a “normal” lot and hope. The two disciplined alternatives are fixed dollar sizing (risk the same amount on every trade) and fixed percentage sizing (risk the same share of your account on every trade). They sound almost identical, but over hundreds of trades they behave very differently: one scales with your account and one does not. This guide explains both, shows the math that separates them, and tells you which to use depending on where you are.

The two ways traders size a position

Once you have decided where your stop goes, you still have to decide how many shares, lots or contracts to buy. There are two principled ways to answer that. Fixed dollar sizing means you risk the same cash amount on every trade — say $200 — no matter how big or small your account is. Fixed percentage sizing (also called fixed fractional) means you risk the same percentage of your current account — say 1% — so the dollar amount rises as you win and shrinks as you lose. Both fix your risk in advance, which already puts you ahead of traders who eyeball position size. The difference is what they fix it to: a static number, or a number that breathes with your equity.

Fixed dollar sizing: simple but blind

Risking a flat dollar amount is the easiest method to run. You decide you will lose no more than $200 if a trade hits its stop, and you size every position to that number — done. It is transparent and predictable, which makes it a reasonable starting point for a brand-new trader still learning the mechanics. The weakness is that it ignores your account. After you double your balance, $200 is now a trivial fraction of your equity and you are massively under-risking — leaving growth on the table. After a drawdown, that same $200 is a dangerously large slice of a shrunken account, so you actually risk more (in percentage terms) exactly when you can least afford it. Fixed dollar sizing is static while your account is not.

Fixed percentage sizing: the compounding default

Risking a fixed percentage solves the problem fixed dollar sizing creates. If you always risk 1% of your account, your bet size grows automatically as you win and contracts automatically as you lose. This does two valuable things. First, it compounds: gains get reinvested into larger positions, so a positive edge snowballs instead of plodding along at a flat dollar rate. Second, it is self-protecting: every loss shrinks the next position, so a losing streak can never produce the sudden account-ending hit that flat sizing allows — the math makes ruin far less likely. The cost is a little more arithmetic per trade, because the dollar amount changes as your balance does. A position size calculator removes that friction entirely: enter your account, your percentage and your stop distance, and it returns the exact size every time.

Which to use — and the mistakes that undo both

For almost everyone trading a growing or shrinking account, fixed percentage wins: it compounds your edge and caps your risk of ruin in the same stroke, which is why it is the standard among professional traders. Fixed dollar sizing has a narrow niche — very large accounts where you deliberately cap absolute risk, or beginners who want maximum simplicity for their first months. Whichever you pick, two mistakes undo the whole exercise. The first is sizing off a round lot instead of your stop: real position size must come from the distance to your stop, never a habit. The second is picking a percentage that is too high — even a great edge can be ruined by risking 5–10% per trade, because variance produces deeper losing streaks than people expect. Most professionals keep it between 0.5% and 2%; run your numbers through a risk of ruin calculator before you trust a larger figure.

For most traders, fixed percentage (fixed fractional) sizing is better. Because it scales with your account, it compounds your gains and automatically shrinks your bet during drawdowns, which lowers your risk of ruin. Fixed dollar sizing is simpler and fine for beginners or for deliberately capping absolute risk on a very large account, but it under-risks after a winning run and over-risks after a losing one.
