What Is a Trailing Stop? When to Use One (and When Not To)

A trailing stop follows the price in your favour and never moves back — locking in gains as a trade runs while capping the loss if it turns. Here is how the different trailing methods work, when a trailing stop beats a fixed one, and the one mistake that gets you shaken out for nothing.

Most traders are good at getting into trades and terrible at getting out. They take a 1R loss cleanly but cut a winner at +1R that goes on to +5R — leaving the trades that were supposed to pay for all the losers on the table. A trailing stop is the mechanical answer to that problem: a stop-loss that ratchets along behind the price as the trade moves in your favour, locking in more and more of the move while staying out of the way until the trend actually ends. Used well, it lets winners run without you having to guess the top. Used badly — set too tight — it does the opposite, ejecting you on ordinary noise before the move even gets going. This guide covers exactly what a trailing stop is, the four ways to set one, when it beats a fixed target, and how to keep it loose enough to survive.

A trailing stop moves with you, never against you

A normal (fixed) stop-loss sits at one price for the life of the trade. A trailing stop is different: it starts at the same place but follows the price whenever the trade moves in your favour, and — critically — never moves the other way. Buy at $100 with a $2 trailing stop and your stop starts at $98. If price rises to $105, the stop ratchets up to $103, locking in $3 of profit. If price then falls, the stop stays at $103 — it does not loosen back to $98. You are stopped out at $103 with a gain, instead of riding the whole move back to a loss. That one-way ratchet is the whole point: it converts an open profit into a protected one without you having to watch the screen or decide when to exit. The trade closes itself the moment the move it was riding reverses by your chosen distance.

Four ways to set the trail

There are four common methods, from crudest to smartest. Fixed amount: trail by a set dollar/point distance (e.g. $2 below the high) — simple but blind to volatility. Fixed percentage: trail by a percent of price (e.g. 8% below the high) — adapts as price grows but still ignores the instrument's actual noise. ATR / volatility-based: trail by a multiple of the Average True Range (e.g. 2–3× ATR below the high), so the distance breathes with the market — wider when the instrument is choppy, tighter when it is calm. This is the most robust because it gives genuine moves room while still reacting to a real reversal. Structure-based (chandelier / swing): trail the stop just under each successive higher swing low (for a long), so you stay in as long as the trend keeps making higher lows and exit the moment that structure breaks. Most disciplined traders use ATR or structure; the fixed-amount and fixed-percent versions are fine for a first pass but get whipsawed in volatile names.

When a trailing stop beats a fixed target

A fixed take-profit target answers "how much do I want?"; a trailing stop answers "how do I stay in until the move is actually over?". Use a trailing stop when you are trading trends or momentum and the upside is open-ended — you do not know how far the move will run, and capping it at a fixed 2R would leave the fat tail on the table. Use a fixed target instead when you are trading a range, a mean-reversion setup, or a level the price is likely to stall at — there, locking in a defined reward beats giving it back waiting for more. Many traders combine the two: take partial profit at a fixed target to bank something, then trail the remainder to let it run. The trailing stop is a tool for the trades where being greedy — in a disciplined, rule-based way — is exactly right.

The trap: too tight and the noise stops you out

The single most common trailing-stop mistake is setting it too close. A trail tighter than the instrument's normal pullback guarantees you get shaken out on ordinary noise — every healthy trend retraces, and a 2% trail on a stock that routinely pulls back 5% will eject you on the first wobble, usually right before the trend resumes without you. The fix is the same logic as fixed-stop placement: size the trail to volatility, not to your impatience. Give the trade room to breathe — a 2.5–3× ATR trail, or the most recent swing low, leaves space for normal retracements while still exiting on a genuine reversal. Also avoid trailing too early: many traders give the trade an initial fixed stop until it is clearly in profit (say +1R), then switch to trailing — so they are not ratcheting a stop on a trade that has not proven itself yet. Loose enough to survive the noise, tight enough to bank the trend: that balance is the entire skill.
