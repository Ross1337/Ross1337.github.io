What Is a Good Win Rate in Trading?

It is the question every new trader asks — and the wrong one to fix on. A 90% win rate can still blow up your account, and a 35% win rate can compound for years. Here is what win rate really tells you, and the number you should track instead.

Ask a room of beginners what makes a profitable trader and most will say a high win rate. It is an intuitive belief and a dangerous one. Win rate — the percentage of your trades that close in profit — feels like the scoreboard of trading, but on its own it is almost meaningless. A trader who wins 90% of the time and lets the rare loser run can lose money; a trader who wins 35% of the time and cuts losses short while riding winners can get rich. This guide explains what win rate actually measures, why there is no universal “good” number, how it trades off against your reward-to-risk, and the single figure that decides whether you are profitable — so you stop chasing a high win rate and start chasing an edge.

What win rate actually measures

Your win rate is simply the share of your trades that end in a profit: 45 winners out of 100 trades is a 45% win rate. That is all it captures — how often you are right, not how much you make when you are right or how much you lose when you are wrong. Because it ignores the size of wins and losses, win rate tells you nothing about profitability by itself. A strategy can be right most of the time and still bleed money if its few losers are large, and it can be wrong most of the time and thrive if its winners dwarf its losers. Treat win rate as one input among several, never as the headline number — the moment you optimise for win rate alone, you start taking tiny profits and holding big losses, which is exactly how most accounts die.

Why there is no universal “good” win rate

There is no single win rate that marks a good trader, because the right number depends entirely on your reward-to-risk ratio. Different, perfectly profitable styles sit at wildly different win rates. A scalper taking quick 1:1 trades might need to win 55–60% to make it pay. A trend follower riding 1:5 winners can be deeply profitable winning just 30% of the time, because the rare big winner covers a long line of small losers. Professional managers and famous trend-following funds often win well under half their trades and still compound for decades. So a “high” win rate of 70% is not automatically better than a “low” one of 35% — it is only better if the reward-to-risk behind it produces a bigger edge. Comparing two traders by win rate alone is like comparing two cars by top speed without knowing the distance.

Win rate vs reward-to-risk: the trade-off

Win rate and reward-to-risk are two ends of the same see-saw. Strategies with high win rates almost always have low reward-to-risk — you bank lots of small, frequent winners but the occasional loss is comparatively large. Strategies with low win rates tend to have high reward-to-risk — you take many small losses while waiting for a few outsized winners. Neither is superior; what matters is that the pairing is positive. The bridge between the two is the break-even win rate: the minimum win rate that keeps a given reward-to-risk profitable, calculated as 1 ÷ (1 + reward-to-risk). At 1:1 you need to win more than 50%; at 1:2, more than 33%; at 1:3, more than 25%. Clear your break-even win rate and you have an edge regardless of whether the number looks high or low. Fall below it and even a “great” win rate loses money.

The number to track instead: expectancy

The figure that actually decides whether you make money is expectancy — your average profit or loss per trade across many trades. It folds win rate and reward-to-risk into one honest number: expectancy = (win% × average win) − (loss% × average loss). A positive expectancy means that, over a large enough sample, your strategy makes money; a negative one means it loses, no matter how good any single trade or win rate looks. This is why expectancy, not win rate, is the metric professionals live by. A 35% win rate with a 1:3 reward-to-risk has a positive expectancy and compounds; an 80% win rate where the occasional loss is five times your average win has a negative one and slowly drains you. Track expectancy over at least 50–100 trades, watch it rather than your hit rate, and you will optimise for the thing that actually grows an account.

Frequently asked questions

There is no universal number, but many profitable day traders sit somewhere between 40% and 60%. What matters far more than the figure is how it pairs with your reward-to-risk: a 45% win rate is excellent at 1:2 and losing at 1:1. Rather than aiming for a target win rate, find the win rate your strategy genuinely produces, then confirm it clears the break-even win rate for your reward-to-risk. If it does, you have an edge; if it does not, raising the win rate or the reward-to-risk is the fix.
