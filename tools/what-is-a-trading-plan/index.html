What Is a Trading Plan? Definition, Components & Template | TradeForge

A trading plan is not a prediction — it is a set of predefined rules covering entries, risk management, position management and routine. Learn the four components every plan needs, the difference between a plan and an idea, and how a trading journal closes the loop.

Every beginner confuses having a trade idea with having a trading plan. The idea is hope dressed up as conviction — "I think Tesla is going to bounce here." The plan is a set of rules written before the market opens that answers four questions: what are the exact conditions for entering? How much am I risking, where is my stop and what is my reward target? How do I manage the position once I am in — partials, trailing, or riding to target? And what is the routine before and after the session? A trading plan does not predict the market; it removes decisions from the moment of the trade, when emotion is highest and judgment is worst. This guide breaks down the four components every trading plan needs, why a "good idea" is not a plan, and how a trading journal closes the loop between intent and execution.

The four components of a real trading plan

A complete trading plan covers exactly four areas and nothing else. First, entry conditions — what setup, on what timeframe, in what market conditions. "Buy if price breaks above resistance with volume" is a condition; "I think it will go up" is not. Second, risk management — the exact stop level, position size, maximum risk per trade and per day, and the minimum reward-to-risk ratio you will accept. Third, position management — whether you take partial profits, trail the stop, or hold until target, and what triggers each decision. Fourth, routine — your pre-session preparation (scanning, level-setting), your in-trade protocol (where you check price, what you do when a trade goes against you), and your post-session review (what you log in your journal, what questions you ask). Every rule in these four boxes should be testable: you should be able to look back at your journal and say "yes, I followed my plan" or "no, I broke rule X." If a rule is not testable, it is not a rule — it is a wish.

Why a plan is not a prediction

This is the hardest lesson for new traders: a trading plan does not try to forecast the market. It accepts that you cannot know where price will go next, and instead controls the things you can control — how much you risk, where you get out, how you size the bet. A good plan works across all market conditions because it accounts for being wrong. Every entry is paired with a stop, every position sized so a series of losses does not cripple the account, and every session followed by a review that feeds back into improvement. A prediction says "I think price will reach 05"; a plan says "If price reaches 05 I will take profit, if it reaches 8 I will exit, and either outcome was accounted for before I entered." The difference is not subtle — it is the difference between gambling and trading.

How a trading journal closes the plan loop

A trading plan is only as good as your ability to follow it and improve it. That is where the journal comes in. Without a journal, every day is disconnected: you have no record of whether you actually executed the plan, no data on which rules you broke and how often, and no way to measure whether the plan itself is profitable. A journal turns the plan into a closed feedback loop. Log every trade with the plan's rules as your checklist — did this entry meet my conditions? Did I use the correct stop? Did I take the partial at the right level? Over time, the journal reveals two things: whether your plan is profitable (via expectancy) and whether you are actually following it (via plan-adherence metrics). The mistake: confusing a trade idea with a trading plan

Most traders who say "I have a plan" are really saying "I have a bias." A bias is directional — you think the market will go up. A plan is conditional — you have written down what needs to happen for the trade to be valid, what invalidates it, and what you will do in every scenario. The tell is simple: if you change your stop or your target because of something you saw after entering, you did not have a plan — you had an idea you improvised on. The cure is to write the plan before you enter, then execute it mechanically. Good entries on a bad plan teach you nothing. Bad entries on a good plan teach you everything. The journal shows you which is which.
