What Is a Trading Strategy? Definition, Components & Backtesting | TradeForge

A trading strategy is a set of mechanical rules for entering and exiting trades. Learn the four components every strategy needs — entry, exit, risk, and sizing — the difference between mechanical and discretionary approaches, and why you test before you trade.

"Buy low, sell high" is not a strategy — it is a wish. A real trading strategy is a set of predefined, mechanical rules that tell you exactly when to enter a trade, when to exit it, how much to risk, and how to size the position. It removes guesswork from the moment of execution, when emotion is highest. A strategy can be purely mechanical (a specific pattern with a fixed stop and target) or partly discretionary (a setup with room for judgment on execution), but in both cases the rules must be clear enough that another trader could follow them without asking questions. This guide breaks down the four components every strategy needs, the difference between mechanical and discretionary approaches, why backtesting separates reality from fantasy, and how the strategy fits into the larger system — plan, backtest, journal — that lets you measure and compound your edge.

What a trading strategy actually is — and is not

A trading strategy is a decision framework that predefines every variable you can control: the conditions for entry, the rules for exit (both profit target and stop-loss), the maximum risk per trade, and the position sizing method. It is not a market opinion, a bias, or a "feeling" about where price might go. The defining feature of a real strategy is repeatability: if you gave your rules to another trader, they could execute a trade from them without calling you. If your rules require interpretation, they are guidelines, not a strategy. A strategy does not need to be complicated to be effective — some of the most robust strategies are a handful of lines. What matters is that the rules are unambiguous, that they are followed consistently, and that their edge is measured over a large enough sample to be trusted.

Mechanical vs discretionary strategies

Mechanical strategies, also called systematic or rule-based, have zero ambiguity: "Buy when the 10 EMA crosses above the 30 EMA on the hourly chart, place a stop 1 ATR below the entry, take profit at 2R." Every parameter is fixed. The advantage is that backtesting is straightforward — the rules are codeable — and execution removes emotion because there are no decisions to make. The disadvantage is that markets change, and a purely mechanical strategy that never adapts can stop working when the regime shifts. Discretionary strategies use rules as a framework but leave room for judgment — an experienced trader might override an entry if the context does not feel right, or adjust a stop based on recent volatility. The advantage is flexibility; the cost is that performance depends on the trader, not just the system, making it harder to distinguish discipline from luck. Most professionals operate somewhere in the middle: a mechanical core with disciplined discretion around execution.

The four components: entry, exit, risk, sizing

Every complete strategy has exactly four components. Entry conditions define the exact market circumstances that trigger a trade — a breakout above resistance, a moving average crossover, a specific candle pattern at a Fibonacci level. Without a precise entry rule, you are gambling, not trading. Exit conditions cover both directions: the profit target where you take the gain and the stop-loss where you admit the trade is invalid. The stop is the most important number in the entire strategy because it defines your R — the unit of risk everything else is measured against. Risk management sets the maximum you are prepared to lose on the trade and per day, ensuring a series of losses cannot cripple your account. Position sizing turns the risk percentage into an actual number of shares or contracts based on the distance to your stop. A strategy missing any of these four components is incomplete; trading it is a hope, not a plan.

Why you test before you trade — and how the journal closes the loop

A strategy on paper is a hypothesis. Until it has been tested, you have no idea whether it works. The first test is a backtest — running your rules against historical data. A backtest tells you the strategy's win rate, expectancy, profit factor and maximum drawdown over a large sample, so you can assess whether the edge is real before you risk capital. But a backtest is not the final answer: it suffers from look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, and the gap between simulated and real execution. The second step is a forward test (paper trading) where you execute the strategy in real time with fake money. Only after a forward test of 50—100 trades should you consider risking small real capital. The loop closes with a trading journal: log every trade against your strategy's rules, measure whether you followed them, and track the real expectancy and R-multiple your strategy produces. Frequently asked questions

A trading strategy is the mechanical part — the entry and exit rules, the indicators or patterns you use. A trading plan is broader: it includes the strategy, plus risk management (position sizing, maximum risk per day), position management (partials, trailing), and the human side (pre-session routine, post-session review). The plan governs how you execute the strategy. A good strategy run without a plan can still lose money; a good plan can make a mediocre strategy profitable by keeping you consistent.
