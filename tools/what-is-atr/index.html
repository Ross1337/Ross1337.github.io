What Is ATR (Average True Range) in Trading?

ATR measures how much an instrument typically moves over a period — its average true range, usually over the last 14 bars. Here is what ATR actually measures, why it lets you size stops and positions to volatility instead of guessing, how it ties volatility, stop placement and position size together, and the mistake of using one fixed stop distance across every instrument.

A 50-cent stop is tight on a sleepy utility stock and laughably tight on a crypto pair that swings 5% before lunch. The same dollar distance means completely different things on different instruments, in different regimes — which is exactly the problem ATR solves. Average True Range is a single number that tells you how far an instrument typically travels in one bar: its average range over the last N periods (14 by default). It is not a direction signal and it says nothing about where price is going. It answers one question — how much does this thing move? — and once you have that number, you can place stops, size positions and set targets that breathe with the market instead of against it.

What ATR actually measures: average true range

ATR is the average of the "true range" over a set number of bars, usually 14. True range is just the largest of three distances on a given bar: the high minus the low, the high minus the previous close, and the previous close minus the low. The reason it uses the previous close, not only the current bar's high-low, is to capture gaps — an instrument that closes at $100 and opens at $95 has really moved $5, even if its bar after the gap is narrow. Average those true ranges over the last 14 bars and you get the ATR: a smoothed reading of typical movement in the same units as price. An ATR of $2 on a $100 stock means it routinely travels about $2 per bar; an ATR of $0.10 means it barely moves. Crucially, ATR is volatility, not direction — a high ATR tells you the instrument is moving a lot, never which way.

Why size stops and positions to volatility, not a fixed number

A stop has to sit far enough from your entry that normal noise does not knock you out, but close enough that being wrong stays cheap. "Normal noise" is exactly what ATR measures, so it is the natural ruler. A stop set at 1.5–2× ATR sits beyond the instrument's typical wiggle: in a calm market that might be a tight 30 cents, in a volatile one a wide $3, and you get stopped only when price moves more than it usually does — a real signal that your idea is wrong, not just routine churn. A fixed stop ignores all of this. Pick a flat "I always use a $1 stop" and on a quiet name you are giving away room for no reason, while on a volatile name a $1 stop is inside the noise and gets clipped on almost every trade. ATR makes the stop adapt to the instrument and the moment instead of forcing every market into one arbitrary distance.

How ATR ties volatility, stop distance and position size together

These three are one chain, and ATR is the first link. Your stop distance comes from ATR (say 2× ATR); your position size then comes from that stop distance, because risk per trade is fixed. The position-sizing rule never changes: size = (account × risk %) ÷ stop distance. So when ATR is high, your stop is wide, and a wide stop forces a smaller position to keep the dollar risk constant; when ATR is low, the stop is tight and you can carry more size for the same risk. This is the elegant part — volatility automatically shrinks your size in wild markets and lets it grow in calm ones, all while the amount you risk per trade stays flat. A trader who sizes off ATR is implicitly taking smaller bets exactly when the market is most dangerous, without ever thinking about it. Feed your ATR-based stop into a position size calculator and the whole chain runs on one input.

The mistake: one fixed stop distance across every instrument

The classic error is carrying a single stop distance — in dollars, ticks, or a flat percentage — from one instrument to the next as if they all moved the same amount. They do not. A 1% stop might be three times the daily range on a large-cap index and a third of it on a small-cap or a crypto pair; the same 1% is far too tight on one and far too loose on the other. Traders who do this get stopped out constantly on volatile names (the stop sits inside normal noise) and bleed too much on quiet ones (the stop is wider than it needs to be, so each loss is bigger than necessary). ATR fixes it by quoting every stop in the instrument's own units of movement: "1.5× ATR" is tight on a calm market and wide on a wild one automatically. The fix is not to find one magic stop distance — it is to stop using a fixed distance at all and let volatility set the scale.
