What Is Drawdown in Trading (and How to Recover from It)?

A drawdown is how far your account has fallen from its peak — and the deeper it gets, the more punishingly hard it becomes to climb back. Here is what drawdown really measures, why recovery is asymmetric, and how to keep it survivable.

Every trader who lasts long enough meets a drawdown. It is the stretch where your account sits below its previous high-water mark, and it is the single most emotionally and mathematically dangerous phase of trading — not because losing money is unusual, but because most traders badly underestimate how hard a deep hole is to climb out of. A 50% drawdown does not need a 50% gain to recover; it needs 100%. That asymmetry is the whole story of drawdown, and the reason “don’t lose big” beats “win big” for long-term survival. This guide explains what drawdown is, the different ways it is measured, the brutal math of recovery, and the practical levers that keep your drawdowns shallow enough to come back from.

What a drawdown actually is

A drawdown is the decline in your account from a peak (its high-water mark) to a subsequent trough, usually expressed as a percentage. If your account grows to $10,000 and then falls to $8,000, you are in a 20% drawdown. The drawdown is only “closed” once you make a new equity high — until then you are still underwater relative to where you once were. It is the most honest measure of pain in trading because it captures the real experience of holding a losing position or enduring a losing streak: not the final return, but how far down you went to get there. Two strategies can end the year up 30% while one quietly compounded and the other plunged 40% mid-year — and only the drawdown tells you which one you could actually have stayed invested in.

Maximum drawdown vs current drawdown

There are two numbers traders track. Your current drawdown is how far below your peak you are right now. Your maximum drawdown (max DD) is the largest peak-to-trough fall your account has ever suffered over a given period — the worst it has ever been. Maximum drawdown is the headline risk statistic for any strategy or track record, because it answers the question that returns alone cannot: what is the worst this has put its owner through? A strategy returning 25% a year with a 15% max drawdown is a very different proposition from one returning 25% with a 60% max drawdown, even though their average returns match. Max drawdown is also a reality check on leverage and position sizing — a large historical drawdown is a warning that the same approach could deliver an even larger one you have not seen yet.

The brutal math of recovery

Here is the part that catches everyone: the gain you need to recover from a drawdown is always larger than the drawdown itself, and the gap explodes as the hole deepens. The formula is recovery gain = d / (1 − d). Lose 10% and you need 11.1% to get back. Lose 25% and you need 33%. Lose 50% and you need a full 100% — your money must double just to break even. Lose 75% and you need 300%. At a 90% loss you need a 900% gain, which is effectively never coming back. This is why a deep drawdown is not just twice as bad as a shallow one — it is exponentially worse, because you are now compounding from a smaller base and need a far bigger percentage move on that shrunken capital. The practical lesson is stark: avoiding deep drawdowns matters more than chasing big returns, because the math of recovery is rigged against you the further down you fall.

How to keep drawdowns survivable

You cannot eliminate drawdowns — they are the cost of taking risk — but you can keep them shallow enough to recover from. The most powerful lever is the same one that governs risk of ruin: risk per trade. Sizing every position at a small fixed percentage (the common 0.5%–2% rule) caps how fast a losing streak can dig the hole, and because the bet automatically shrinks as the account falls, fixed-percentage sizing naturally brakes a drawdown instead of accelerating it. Beyond sizing, three habits help: keep a genuinely positive expectancy (a losing edge guarantees the drawdown never recovers), avoid concentrated or correlated bets that all lose together, and set a personal maximum-drawdown limit at which you cut size or stop to reassess — treating a deep drawdown as a signal, not a dare. When you do take a hit, use the recovery math to size the climb back realistically rather than trying to win it all back fast with oversized trades, which is exactly how a recoverable drawdown becomes a fatal one.

Frequently asked questions

It depends on your risk tolerance and strategy, but most traders treat anything beyond 20–30% as a serious warning sign, and professional managers often aim to keep maximum drawdown in the single digits to low teens. The reason is the recovery math: a 20% drawdown needs a 25% gain to recover, which is achievable, while a 50% drawdown needs a 100% gain, which is not realistic for most. A lower maximum drawdown means you can stay in the game and let your edge compound rather than spending years just getting back to even.
