What Is an R-Multiple in Trading?

An R-multiple expresses the result of a trade in units of the risk you took, not in dollars: 1R is the amount you put at risk, and every outcome is measured against it. Here is why thinking in R makes wildly different trades comparable, how it ties together sizing, expectancy and win rate, and the mistake of judging a trade by its dollar result.

Two traders close out the week. One made $500 on a single trade, the other made $50. Who traded better? You cannot say — not until you know what each of them risked to get there. If the first risked $1,000 to make $500 and the second risked $25 to make $50, the "small" trade was twice as efficient. That is the entire reason R-multiples exist. R is simply your initial risk on a trade — the distance from entry to stop, multiplied by your size — and the R-multiple is the result divided by that risk. It turns every trade, no matter the instrument, size or account, into the same currency: units of risk. Once you think in R, sizing, expectancy and win rate stop being separate ideas and become one coherent system.

R is your initial risk — the unit everything is measured in

R stands for risk: the amount you stand to lose on a trade if it hits your stop. If you buy a stock at $50 with a stop at $48 and a position of 100 shares, your 1R is the $2 distance times 100 shares = $200. That $200 is your unit. A trade that makes $400 is a +2R winner; one that loses the full stop is a −1R loser; one you exit early for a $100 gain is +0.5R. The dollar amounts change every time you size differently, but the R-multiple does not — it always answers the same question: how many multiples of what I risked did this trade return? Defining 1R before you enter also forces the two decisions that actually matter — where your stop goes and how big your position is — to happen up front, instead of being rationalised after the trade moves.

Why thinking in R beats thinking in dollars

Dollars are noisy. A $300 win means something completely different on a $2,000 account than on a $200,000 one, and a scalp risking $40 is not comparable to a swing risking $4,000 when both are measured in cash. R strips all of that away. A +3R day is a +3R day whether you traded micro size or full size — so you can finally compare a Tuesday scalp to a Friday swing, this month to last month, this strategy to that one, on equal terms. R also reframes losses healthily: a −1R loss is just "one unit," the cost you pre-agreed to pay for the shot, not a scary dollar figure that tempts you to move your stop. And because every trade is normalised to the same unit, you can string them together and actually measure your edge — which dollars alone never let you do cleanly.

How R ties sizing, expectancy and win rate together

R is the thread that connects every other risk concept. Position sizing is just the act of deciding how many dollars equal 1R — risk a fixed 1% of your account per trade and 1R is always 1% of equity, so your results read directly in R regardless of price or volatility. Expectancy is your average R per trade: add up the R-multiples of all your trades and divide by the number of trades, and you get the average number of risk-units you make (or lose) every time you pull the trigger. A system with +0.4R expectancy makes, on average, four-tenths of a risk-unit per trade — a number you can multiply by how often you trade to project growth. Win rate and reward-to-risk are simply the two inputs that produce that expectancy: expectancy = (win rate × average win in R) − (loss rate × average loss in R). Think in R and these stop being four disconnected metrics and become one chain — size sets R, each trade returns an R-multiple, their average is your expectancy, and win rate and reward decide whether that average is positive.

The mistake: judging a trade by its dollar result

The most common error R fixes is grading trades by how much money they made rather than how much risk they took to make it. A trader brags about a $2,000 winner that, on inspection, risked $4,000 with no stop — a +0.5R outcome that got lucky and could just as easily have been a −5R disaster. Another quietly logs a string of clean +2R and +3R winners on small size and is told they are "not making real money." Judged in dollars, the first looks like the better trader; judged in R, the second is running a far better process and will pull ahead the moment they size up. Dollars reward outcome and size; R rewards decision quality and consistency. The goal is not to maximise the dollars on any single trade — it is to keep taking high-R-multiple setups with controlled 1R risk, and let position size, applied to a positive expectancy, turn that into dollars over time.
